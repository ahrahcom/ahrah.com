Rescue

Rescue the live product or start over

By Harsika 1 November 2026 8 min read

For teams with a product in production that is slow, fragile, or no longer understood.

The rewrite is tempting because it feels like a clean start. New stack, new team, new promises. Meanwhile customers are already on the old system, money is already moving through it, and the people who remember why the edges exist have left. A full rewrite is a decision, not the default. The default is to stabilize what is live, harden the paths customers depend on, and ship the next version on purpose.

Starting over is sometimes honest. It is honest when you cannot make the product safe, when you cannot change it without weeks of archaeology, or when the commercial shape has changed so much that the old system is answering the wrong job. It is not honest when the team is tired, the vendor is unfashionable, or a new engineer wants a portfolio piece.

What rescue actually means

Rescue is not a polish pass. It is making the current product operable: the core job works, you can deploy a fix, you can see failures, and you are no longer one person away from a weekend outage. After that, you choose the next slice — a faster path, a cleaner permission model, a module you replace because it is the actual fire.

That is different from “rebuild everything behind a feature flag and hope we cut over in Q4.” Dual running two products is a company, not a sprint. If you do not have the people to operate both, you do not have a rewrite plan. You have a wish.

Ahrah’s rescue and modernization work starts with the live paths, not the architecture diagram. The diagram can wait until the customer-facing job is boring again.

When you keep the live product

Keep it when customers finish a job you still sell, even if the internals are ugly. Keep it when the data model is the business — orders, claims, ledgers, student records — and a rewrite would mean translating years of exceptions. Keep it when you can still ship a change in days once you restore a pipeline and a test around the risky flows.

Ugly is not the same as doomed. Many products look doomed because nobody has drawn the core path, isolated the integrations that fail quietly, or written down how production is deployed. After two weeks of that work, teams often discover they do not need a new product. They need an operable one.

Keep it also when a buyer is in diligence. A rewrite announced in the middle of a security review reads as “we cannot explain the current system.” Buyers would rather see access control, logs, and a recovery story than a promise of a future stack.

When starting over is honest

Start over when you cannot make the product safe enough for the data it holds — not “we should use a newer framework,” but “we cannot tell who can see customer records, and we cannot change that without breaking production.” Start over when the system cannot be understood well enough to change: no tests, no environments, no remaining author, and every fix creates two more defects.

Start over when the product is answering the wrong job. If the market moved from one-off projects to a multi-tenant service, or from internal tool to customer-facing SaaS, you may be extending a shape that will never fit. In that case the rescue is commercial: keep the old system alive for existing users while a new first release proves the new job.

Start over in slices even then. A big-bang cutover is how companies lose the year. Replace the riskiest module, or take new customers onto the new path, and give the old system a retirement date you can defend.

A two-week way to decide

Do not fund a year of rewrite on a workshop. Spend two weeks answering five questions with evidence:

  1. What is the core job customers still pay for, and does it work today?
  2. Can we deploy a change to production, including a rollback, without a hero?
  3. Can a new engineer find where a permission, a payment, or a migration lives?
  4. Which integrations fail quietly, and who owns them?
  5. If we had to make the product safe for a buyer’s questionnaire, what would we not be able to say?

If the answers are painful but finite, rescue. If you cannot answer them at all, or the answers say the product cannot be made safe or shippable in the time you have, write the case for a replacement — including who operates the old system while the new one is built.

The cost people forget

A rewrite has a hidden payroll: every week you run two products, two incident rotations, two places for a bug to hide, and two stories for sales. Customers will still call about the old one. Staff will still know the old one. The new one will be late, because new products are late.

Price that. If the rewrite only works if nothing else happens for nine months, it will not work. Rescue plus a narrow next version is usually the cheaper company, even when it is the less exciting slide.

What you do on Monday

Pick the core job. Restore deploy and visibility. Put tests on the flows that would hurt a customer or a launch. Then choose one of three outcomes in writing: stabilize and continue, stabilize and replace a module, or stabilize and start a new product for the new job. Ambiguous programs are how rewrites last forever.

If the pressure to “just add AI” is part of the panic, read that as a symptom. A model will not save a path you cannot operate. An AI pilot on a fragile host becomes another incident source.
