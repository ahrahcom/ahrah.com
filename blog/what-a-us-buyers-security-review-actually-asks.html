Security

What a US buyer’s security review actually asks

By Harsika 1 November 2026 8 min read

For founders selling into US companies that have a questionnaire, a legal team, and a date.

The spreadsheet looks like bureaucracy. Two hundred rows, three attachments, a request for SOC 2. Underneath it, the buyer is asking a smaller set of questions: who can see our data, how do you change the system, what happens when something breaks, and which other companies are in the path. If you can answer those in plain language, with evidence, you will survive most reviews. If you only have a logo on a slide, you will stall the deal.

Ahrah is not a cybersecurity audit firm and does not claim SOC 2, ISO 27001, or GDPR certification in its own name. That distinction matters. Buyers can tell the difference between a certificate you hold and engineering that will not embarrass you when they ask how production actually works. This note is about the second thing: being able to explain the product you already run.

They are not buying the badge

A SOC 2 report, when you have one, is a useful shortcut. It is not the review. Plenty of early products win US deals without it by being specific: here is the data we store, here is the region, here is who has admin, here is the last incident and what changed. Plenty of later products lose time because the report exists and nobody in the room can map it to the live system.

Treat the questionnaire as a conversation about your product, not a hunt for the word “yes.” “Yes, we encrypt” without saying what, where, and who holds the keys is a shrug. “No, we do not have SOC 2 yet; here is how access, logging, and backups work today” is often enough for a commercial team that wants to buy.

Who can see customer data

This is the first real question. They want to know which roles exist, how you grant them, whether staff can open a customer record in production, and whether that access is logged. They will ask about your own people, contractors, and anyone offshore. Geography is not a moral test. It is a data-flow test. Say where work is done and how production access is limited.

They will also ask how a customer’s user is kept out of another customer’s data. If you cannot describe tenancy in a paragraph, you do not have an answer yet. That is a product problem, not a paperwork problem. If the live system cannot be explained, you may need to rescue it before you sell it into a sharper buyer.

Passwords, single sign-on, session timeout, and what happens when someone leaves the company belong here. The buyer has been burned by accounts that never expire. Have a list of admin users you can produce without a hunt.

How you ship a change

Security reviewers have learned that most incidents are self-inflicted: a change, a permission, a migration. They will ask who can deploy, whether reviews happen, whether secrets live in the repository, and whether you can roll back. This is the same standard as a first release you can deploy again tomorrow, written as diligence.

You do not need a novel. You need a short description of the path from a pull request to production, the checks that run, and who is on the hook if the release misbehaves. If the honest answer is “someone copies files onto a server,” say so and fix that before you promise enterprise customers a weekly ship.

What you do when it breaks

They will ask about backups, how often you test a restore, how you hear about an outage, and how you tell customers. They will ask whether you have had an incident and what you changed afterward. “We have never had one” from a product that has been live for two years is less comforting than a short write-up of a real event.

Logging sits here too. Can you tell who exported a file last month? Can you tell what a support engineer did in a customer workspace? If the product includes AI, can you tell what was sent to a model vendor and whether that data is retained? Those questions now show up on the same form as encryption. An AI feature that was never treated as a release is hard to defend.

Which vendors sit in the path

Hosting, email, payments, analytics, error tracking, model APIs, support tools — the buyer wants the list, the data each one sees, and whether you have a contract that matches. They are not trying to redesign your stack. They are mapping their risk to yours.

Keep a current vendor list. Note the region, the data category, and whether the vendor trains on customer content. That last point is now a default question for anything with a model in it. “We pasted production data into a consumer chatbot to debug” is an answer you do not want to give.

What to prepare before the form arrives

  • A one-page data map: what you collect, where it lives, who can open it, how long you keep it.
  • An access list for production and admin tools, with a leavers process.
  • A short description of how you ship, test, and roll back.
  • Backup and restore: last successful test, not only the schedule.
  • An incident contact and a customer-notice outline.
  • A vendor list with data and region, including AI providers.
  • Honest wording for the certifications you do not have yet.

That packet is enough for many mid-market reviews. It also makes a later audit cheaper, because you are not inventing the system under a deadline. Secure delivery at Ahrah means aligning the build with those expectations so a later audit is not a rewrite — not claiming a certificate we do not hold.

If you are still pre-revenue, do not wait for the first US enterprise deal to invent this. The first release is the cheapest moment to decide where data lives and who can see it. The expensive moment is the week legal forwards a portal login and your only environment is a laptop.
